Eleven Winters

Every winter, Delhi blames the weather, the farm fires in Punjab and Haryana, and Diwali. They arrive together, so nobody can say how much each one adds. We used the years when they didn't line up to pull them apart.

The design

Three things that change from year to year

Diwali follows the lunar calendar, so it moves across the burning season. In 2017 it fell on 19 October, before most farm fires. In 2020 it fell on 14 November, at their height. Delhi's firecracker rules changed four times in the same decade. And the timing of crop burning itself appears to have shifted. These changes let us compare like with like.

A · Weather

How much of the winter is weather?

For every monitor, a machine-learning model learns how PM2.5 responds to temperature, humidity, wind, rain and the height of the mixed layer of air near the ground. Re-running it hundreds of times with the weather shuffled gives the pollution each site would have had under typical weather.

{% if n.gap_n_sites %}

Most of the north's winter problem is what is emitted, not the weather it is emitted into.

B · Diwali

Diwali night, with the weather and the farm smoke taken out

We built a baseline for every hour: what PM2.5 should have been given that night's weather, the time of year and how much farm smoke the wind was carrying from Punjab and Haryana. The model never sees Diwali, and every hour is predicted by a version of it that never saw that week. Diwali's excess is what the air held above that baseline from 6 pm to 3 am.

In the CPCB network median, Diwali night added between {{ n.diwali_main_min|fmt }} and {{ n.diwali_main_max|fmt }} µg/m³ in every year with data. Among {{ n.diwali_placebo_n|fmt }} ordinary autumn nights scored the same way, the 99th percentile was {{ n.diwali_placebo_p99|fmt }}.

The rules made no visible difference

Mean Diwali-night excess was {{ n.diwali_permissive_mean|fmt }} µg/m³ in years with no effective restriction, {{ n.diwali_green_window_mean|fmt }} with green crackers and {{ n.diwali_full_ban_mean|fmt }} under a full ban. There are only a few Diwalis per regime, so this is not proof that bans cannot work, but it gives no sign that these did.

The monitor that goes dark

The US Embassy monitor is the most-used reference for Delhi. On Diwali it often repeats its last reading and then stops reporting during the worst hours. Late-night hours go missing {{ n.cens_ratio|fmt(0) }} times as often as on an ordinary night, so any Diwali average built on this monitor alone leaves out the peak.

{{ figure("fig08_censoring", figs, "Share of hours missing at the US Embassy monitor from 5 pm to 6 am, Diwali nights against ordinary nights.") }}

A fingerprint of fireworks

On Diwali evening, compared with the same hours a week earlier, the air gets finer and more sulphurous: the PM2.5 to PM10 ratio and SO₂ both rise, while NO₂, mostly from traffic, does not follow. The timing also differs between cities. In Delhi the excess builds into the night. In Chennai, where Deepavali is celebrated at dawn, part of it comes in the morning.

C · Farm fires

Fewer fires counted, but more of them at night

Official progress on stubble burning is measured in satellite fire counts. In Punjab and Haryana, VIIRS detections in October and November fell {{ n.viirs_drop_2021_2025|fmt(0, pct=True) }} between 2021 and 2025. But the same satellite also passes at 1:30 am, and the share of fires it catches then rose from {{ n.night_share_2016_2021|fmt(1, pct=True) }} to {{ n.night_share_2025|fmt(1, pct=True) }}. More fires are still burning in the middle of the night, which is what you would see if farmers now light them after the afternoon pass.

{{ figure("fig11_fires", figs, "Afternoon-pass fire detections fall sharply after 2021 while the night-pass share rises from under one percent to several percent.") }}

Each counted fire now carries more smoke

Delhi's weather-adjusted PM2.5 follows upwind fires over one to four days. From 2016 to 2021, 100 units of the fire index went with about {{ n.coupling_early|fmt(1) }}% more PM2.5. In 2025 the same 100 units went with {{ n.coupling_2025|fmt(0) }}% more. If each detected fire now stands for more burning, that is the pattern you would expect.

{{ figure("fig12_coupling", figs, "Percent rise in Delhi PM2.5 per 100 fire index units by season, with confidence intervals; 2025 stands well above earlier years.") }}

The ground monitors can't confirm it yet

If burning moved to the evening, monitors inside Punjab and Haryana should see more evening pollution during the harvest. They don't, so far. But the open archive is missing 2023 and 2024, and 2025 had so little burning that a change in its timing would barely move a city monitor. We report this as a test that could not yet be run, not as evidence against the shift.

A forecast we can be wrong about
